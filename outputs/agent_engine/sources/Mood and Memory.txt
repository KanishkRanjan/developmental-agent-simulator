Unifying Short-Timescale Affect Dynamics and Long-Timescale Memory Consolidation in Large-Scale Multi-Agent Social Simulations
Executive Summary
The structural unification of a short-timescale affective state (mood) with a long-timescale memory system (power-law forgetting) is an architectural necessity for producing biologically plausible agents in large-scale social simulations. Relying on Large Language Models (LLMs) to infer affective states from raw text logs produces unacceptable persona drift, lacks temporal continuity, and fails to support arbitrary simulation time-skips. The analysis evaluates the integration of these two temporal domains within the constraints of lazy evaluation and event-driven architectures.
The evaluation of the proposed design hypotheses (H1-H8) yields the following determinations, utilizing evidence from affective chronometry, psychometrics, and computational cognitive modeling.

	Evidence Review
Q1. Temporal Dynamics of Affect
(Confidence: Strong)
The temporal unfolding of emotions, formally termed affective chronometry, contradicts the assumption that emotional states decay exponentially back to a baseline. Discrete-time survival analyses of emotion duration reveal that the hazard rate of emotions—defined as the conditional probability that an emotional episode will end in a given time interval—is highly variable across emotion types and durations1. The hazard rate of fear drops steeply and linearly, resulting in a short median lifetime of approximately 16 minutes1. Conversely, emotions such as anger and joy exhibit a distinct hazard function that rises slightly before undergoing a steep drop after one hour, yielding longer median lifetimes of 22 and 26 minutes, respectively1.
Crucially, the survivor functions indicate a heavy-tailed distribution. Emotions that survive the initial hourly intervals are disproportionately likely to endure for significantly longer periods1. This heavy-tailed nature implies that affect recovery is mathematically better modeled by power-law dynamics rather than the memoryless exponential decay commonly implemented in naive computational agents. Furthermore, the duration of an emotional episode is heavily modulated by the initial intensity of the event and the presence of rumination1.
In computational psychology, continuous-time affective modeling frequently relies on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which is the continuous analogue of a first-order autoregressive AR(1) model3. While AR(1) models successfully capture the tendency of a system to revert to a mean baseline, the heavy-tailed real-world data and the compounding effect of rumination imply that acute emotional impulses persist longer than a standard exponential AR(1) process predicts. To capture both the mean-reverting nature of the OU process and the heavy-tailed survival data from affective chronometry, the computational model must utilize dynamic, intensity-dependent time constants governed by power-law impulse formulations.
Q2. Individual Differences
(Confidence: Strong)
Personality traits systematically govern affective dynamics. The Cybernetic Big Five Theory (CB5T) provides a mechanistic framework, positioning traits as parameters of evolved cybernetic mechanisms regulating entropy and goal-directed behavior6. Within CB5T, the meta-trait of Stability (representing the shared variance of Conscientiousness, Agreeableness, and inverse Neuroticism) governs the protection of existing behavioral policies from disruption, while Plasticity (Extraversion and Openness) governs exploration7.
Empirical research on emotional inertia—defined as the degree to which emotional states resist change and carry over from one moment to the next—demonstrates a profound correlation with Neuroticism and psychological maladjustment9. High Neuroticism correlates with high emotional inertia, meaning affective states persist longer and are more self-predictive over time. This inertia is independent of the initial reactivity to a stimulus9. Individuals with high Extraversion exhibit heightened reactivity (initial response amplitude) to positive stimuli, while highly neurotic individuals exhibit heightened reactivity to negative stimuli and systemic uncertainty12. Consequently, reactivity (the amplitude of the emotional impulse) and inertia (the decay rate of the impulse) are distinct, separable mathematical parameters, though both are heavily modulated by the agent's Big Five profile. Furthermore, the cybernetic framework supports co-fluctuation under stress, where acute adversity increases entropy, driving Neuroticism up while suppressing Extraversion and Conscientiousness6.
Q3. Mood and Memory Interaction
(Confidence: Strong for Mood-Congruent and Arousal; Contested for Mood-Dependent)
The interaction between affect and memory operates primarily through two independent, neurobiologically distinct pathways: encoding enhancement and retrieval bias. Regarding encoding, extensive neurobiological literature establishes that emotional arousal activates the basolateral amygdala via noradrenergic pathways, which subsequently modulates memory consolidation in regions such as the hippocampus13. This consolidation modulation mechanism implies that the initial structural stability of a memory trace is a direct mathematical function of the organism's physiological arousal at the exact time of encoding or shortly thereafter. Experiences accompanied by significant adrenal stress hormone release are consolidated into more durable traces16.
Regarding retrieval, a strict distinction must be drawn between mood-dependent memory and mood-congruent memory. Mood-dependent memory (the hypothesis that recall is superior if the retrieval mood matches the encoding mood, regardless of the memory's intrinsic valence) has repeatedly failed rigorous replication and proved highly unreliable in laboratory settings18. It represents folklore that should not be encoded into the architecture. Conversely, mood-congruent memory (the hypothesis that current mood biases the retrieval of memories matching that specific mood valence) is a highly robust phenomenon. Meta-analyses demonstrate a baseline positive bias (  ) in healthy individuals, which severely inverts to a negative bias (  ) in clinically depressed individuals18. This bias operates as a transient filter in healthy individuals but locks into place in maladaptive states. The depressed brain does not merely retrieve negative memories; it actively constructs them, filling gaps with mood-congruent material18. Therefore, current mood must act as a continuous modifier on the retrievability of similarly valenced memories during the querying phase, without altering the underlying consolidated stability.
Q4. Appraisal and Construal
(Confidence: Moderate)
Appraisal theories dictate that emotions are not elicited directly by raw stimuli, but by the subjective appraisal of the stimulus regarding its relevance, implications, and coping potential. Scherer’s Component Process Model positions this appraisal as a sequential, memory-dependent operation20. The construal of a situation relies entirely on the retrieval of episodic precedents to estimate predictability, threat, and goal-congruence.
While the DIAMONDS framework successfully categorizes situations into eight discrete dimensions (Duty, Intellect, Adversity, Mating, pOsitivity, Negativity, Deception, Sociality)22, driving cybernetic mood dynamics exclusively through discrete categories results in a loss of continuous valuation. A continuous appraisal dimension representation—specifically mapping Valence, Arousal, and Controllability—aligns more accurately with the cybernetic parameters of CB5T. To operationalize appraisal, the event classification must query the agent's memory for relevant past experiences; if past betrayals are retrieved during a neutral event, the appraised threat level increases, directly modulating the intensity of the resulting emotional impulse.
Q5. Computational Models
(Confidence: Moderate)
Existing formal models of affect dynamics provide frameworks for coupling cognition and emotion. Reinforcement-learning models, notably the "mood-as-momentum" model proposed by Eldar and Niv, conceptualize mood as an aggregate of recent reward prediction errors25. In these models, positive prediction errors elevate mood, which in turn acts as a ubiquitous bias on future expected values. This bidirectional coupling creates a mathematical feedback loop where mood alters environmental perception, and perception iteratively alters mood.
Cognitive architectures such as ACT-R and affective frameworks like FAtiMA implement PAD-based (Pleasure, Arousal, Dominance) emotional states, typically utilizing linear or standard exponential decay functions. However, none of these standard architectures inherently account for the power-law forgetting curves observed in human memory consolidation, nor are they optimized for the lazy evaluation required by asynchronous, multi-agent LLM simulations. The proposed design must therefore adapt the bidirectional feedback loop of the mood-as-momentum model while replacing exponential decay with heavy-tailed power-law decay.
Q6. LLM-Agent Literature
(Confidence: Moderate)
The simulation of society using LLMs, exemplified by Generative Agents, relies heavily on text-based memory streams, spatial distance heuristics, and periodic reflective consolidation27. While these agents exhibit plausible emergent social behaviors, their emotional states are typically tracked via prompt engineering rather than underlying mathematical formalisms. Relying purely on an LLM to infer its mood from a text log of retrieved memories leads to severe persona drift and a total lack of emotional inertia. The LLM has no temporal decay function native to its context window; a memory from ten years ago and a memory from ten seconds ago carry identical affective weight unless explicitly timestamp-engineered.
Introducing a quantitative, cybernetic mood tracker external to the LLM stabilizes the agent's baseline and ensures biologically plausible affective chronometry. Empirical validation of LLM-simulated affect indicates that while LLMs can accurately classify discrete emotions, they fail to natively simulate the temporal decay of those emotions over simulated time-skips. Grounding the LLM's generative output in strict mathematical constraints provided by an external state vector ensures realistic behavior.

Q8. Failure Modes and Safeguards
(Confidence: Strong)
Coupled dynamical systems of mood and memory are highly susceptible to runaway feedback loops. If an agent retrieves a sad memory, it generates a negative affective impulse; this negative mood biases subsequent retrievals toward further sad memories, infinitely amplifying the state. While this reflects the clinical reality of depressive rumination18, it leads to mathematical divergence (saturation) in a simulation.
Safeguards require specific boundary conditions grounded in cybernetic theory. First, a bounding function, such as the hyperbolic tangent (  ), must strictly limit absolute mood. Second, an adaptation mechanism must be introduced where repeated retrieval of the exact same memory yields diminishing affective impulses, simulating neurological habituation31. Third, an integration ceiling or affective refractory period is required to prevent infinite loops during highly active event clusters.
Q9. Classifier Reliability
(Confidence: Moderate)
Utilizing an LLM to classify every micro-event into eight DIAMONDS dimensions is computationally expensive and highly prone to categorization hallucinations. While DIAMONDS maps robustly to behavioral outcomes in human experience sampling23, reducing event classification to continuous embedding-based classifiers or simpler Valence-Arousal regressions is computationally cheaper and mathematically more reliable. If LLM classification is maintained, constraining the output to a normalized dimensional vector    via strictly typed output formats (e.g., JSON schema enforcement) minimizes parser failures and ensures the mood equations receive bounded, predictable inputs. A continuous appraisal dimension representation (Valence, Arousal, Controllability) is vastly superior for driving cybernetic mood dynamics compared to discrete categorical tags.

Validation of the unified system must be grounded in Whole Trait Theory (WTT), which posits that personality traits are essentially the means of density distributions of momentary states, with approximately 80% of total behavioral variance occurring within-person across situations32. The simulation is successfully validated if, over thousands of varied events, the density distribution of a specific agent's mood states matches the expected mean and standard deviation of their assigned Big Five profile. Python Pseudocode
The following pseudocode outlines the implementation of the lazy evaluation and coupled dynamics. It is designed for integration into event-driven simulation environments.






Python
import math
from typing import List

class MoodImpulse:
   def __init__(self, intensity: float, valence: float, tau: float, timestamp: float):
       self.intensity = intensity
       self.valence = valence
       self.tau = tau
       self.timestamp = timestamp

class Agent:
   def __init__(self, n_score: float, e_score: float):
       self.neuroticism = n_score
       self.extraversion = e_score
       self.impulses: List[MoodImpulse] = []
       
       # Cybernetic trait mappings
       self.m_base = (e_score - 0.5) * 0.5 
       self.inertia_mult = 1.0 + (n_score * 2.0)
       self.reactivity_pos = 1.0 + (e_score * 1.5)
       self.reactivity_neg = 1.0 + (n_score * 1.5)
       self.rumination_mult = n_score

   def compute_mood_lazily(self, current_time: float) -> float:
       """
       Computes current mood exactly at query time (no ticks).
       Utilizes power-law summation and tanh bounding.
       """
       decay_shape_alpha = 0.5
       active_sum = 0.0
       alive_impulses = []
       
       for imp in self.impulses:
           delta_t = current_time - imp.timestamp
           if delta_t < 0:
               continue
           
           # Power-law decay mimicking heavy-tail affective chronometry
           decay_factor = math.pow(1.0 + (delta_t / imp.tau), -decay_shape_alpha)
           impulse_val = imp.intensity * imp.valence * decay_factor
           
           # Culling threshold to prevent array growth
           if abs(impulse_val) > 0.01:
               active_sum += impulse_val
               alive_impulses.append(imp)
               
       self.impulses = alive_impulses # State cleanup
       
       # Tanh bounds the mood to [-1, 1] to prevent runaway divergence
       return math.tanh(self.m_base + active_sum)

   def process_event(self, event_valence: float, event_intensity: float, current_time: float):
       """Creates a new mood impulse based on an external event."""
       # Modulate reactivity by traits
       if event_valence > 0:
           adjusted_intensity = event_intensity * self.reactivity_pos
       else:
           adjusted_intensity = event_intensity * self.reactivity_neg
           
       # Tau (stability) determined by event intensity and agent inertia
       tau = max(1.0, adjusted_intensity * 100.0 * self.inertia_mult)
       
       new_impulse = MoodImpulse(
           intensity=adjusted_intensity,
           valence=event_valence,
           tau=tau,
           timestamp=current_time
       )
       self.impulses.append(new_impulse)

def score_memory_retrieval(agent: Agent, memory, current_time: float) -> float:
   """
   Scores memory retrievability using FSRS and mood-congruent bias.
   Memory object expects attributes: stability, last_retrieved, valence
   """
   delta_t = current_time - memory.last_retrieved
   
   # FSRS 4.5 baseline retrievability
   retrievability = math.pow(1.0 + (19.0/81.0) * (delta_t / memory.stability), -0.5)
   
   current_mood = agent.compute_mood_lazily(current_time)
   
   # Mood-congruent memory bias (Matt & Vazquez, 1992)
   mood_bias_weight = 0.2
   bias = mood_bias_weight * (current_mood * memory.valence)
   
   return max(0.0, min(1.0, retrievability + bias))

def retrieve_and_ruminate(agent: Agent, memory, current_time: float):
   """Retrieving a memory creates a rumination impulse."""
   ret_score = score_memory_retrieval(agent, memory, current_time)
   if ret_score > 0.5: # Threshold for successful retrieval
       rumination_intensity = memory.arousal * agent.rumination_mult * 0.5
       agent.process_event(
           event_valence=memory.valence,
           event_intensity=rumination_intensity,
           current_time=current_time
       )
       memory.last_retrieved = current_time

Open Problems
The literature and this formulation leave specific computational parameters unresolved, which must be calibrated empirically during simulation tuning:
The optimal power-law shape parameter (  ) for affective hazard rates is not mathematically standardized in the literature. While memory decay in FSRS-4.5 utilizes   29, applying this directly to mood impulses may cause emotions to last artificially long in highly volatile environments. Empirical tuning is required to align the parameter with Verduyn's median emotion lifetimes.
Furthermore, using LLMs to continuously determine event valence and intensity introduces latency and LLM bias. It remains entirely untested whether an LLM can consistently output normalized continuous valence/intensity values across thousands of diverse edge cases without artificially drifting toward a "neutral" safe baseline due to RLHF safety training.
Finally, cross-trait interactions remain theoretically complex. The CB5T literature maps traits to broad cybernetic parameters, but precisely how Conscientiousness dampens emotional impulses (regulatory control) versus how Agreeableness alters the valence of social events is theoretically sound but computationally unquantified in current open-source agent frameworks.
